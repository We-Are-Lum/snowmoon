# Visual storytelling: what the planning coach looks for

> **Draft for the owner to edit and approve.** Written by the coding agent
> (Claude, a closed model), 2026-10-11, from the owner's list of principles.
> The coach (`config/prompts/plan-coach.md`) may only use an approved
> version. Status: **not approved**.

This is the craft the coach applies to two forms: **comics with narration**
(the stage 1 format in `docs/adaptation-format.md`: one image per beat, at
most 25 words of narration) and **short audiovisual pieces** (an animatic or
short video timed to narration). The coach uses these principles to ask
questions and write notes. It never writes the piece. Its notes say what to
change and why, never a replacement sentence (see "What the coach never
does" below).

Each principle has three parts:
- **the idea**, with where it comes from;
- **what the coach asks**, one question at a time;
- **what a note looks like**, without any prose for the piece.

Examples point at Snowmoon by block ID, so a writer can see the idea in the
book.

## 1. Each scene has a want, an obstacle and a turn

**The idea.** A scene is worth keeping when someone wants something,
something stands in the way, and by the end the situation has changed for
better or worse. Robert McKee calls this change the scene's *turn*: a value
in the character's life goes from positive to negative, or the reverse
(*Story*, 1997). Lajos Egri argues that conflict between what people want
drives drama (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*, 1946). A beat where nothing
turns is a candidate to cut or merge.

**In the book.** At the concert, Gladias wants to keep his prediction score
above ninety (c1-b68). The crowd and a stranger's shout stand in his way
(c1-b73). The turn: he escapes, but someone now knows who he is (c1-b138).

**The coach asks.** "In this beat, what does your character want, and what
stops them?" "What is different at the end of the scene?"

**A note looks like.** "Beats 3 and 4 have the same want and no turn
between them. Consider merging them, or giving beat 4 an obstacle."

## 2. Show inner states through action and objects

**The idea.** A feeling lands harder when the reader infers it from what a
character does or holds than when the narration names it. T. S. Eliot
called an object or situation that carries an emotion an *objective
correlative* ("Hamlet and His Problems", 1919). The advice often credited
to Anton Chekhov, show the glint of moonlight on broken glass rather than
saying the moon shone, is a popular paraphrase of a letter he wrote to his
brother in 1886. It is cited here for the idea, not as a quotation.

**In the book.** Zei opening and closing each finger of his healed arm
(c4-b51, c9-b75). The dark purple letter he pockets unopened (c7-b141,
c7-b144).

**The coach asks.** "What could your character do or hold that shows this,
so the narration doesn't have to say it?"

**A note looks like.** "Narration in beat 2 names the feeling ('he was
afraid'). The picture could carry it through an action or an object, which
would free those words for something the picture can't show."

## 3. Establish the place before the faces

**The idea.** Readers and viewers need to know where they are before close
shots mean much. In film grammar this is the *establishing shot*: wide
first, then closer (Daniel Arijon, *Grammar of the Film Language*, 1976).
In comics it is the opening panel of a scene that sets the space (Will
Eisner, *Comics and Sequential Art*, 1985). Snowmoon switches between
storylines, so a scene that opens on a face can leave the reader unsure
which country they are in.

**In the book.** Each chapter opens with a place and a date (c1-b1, c2-b1),
then a wide view before people: the Kalimar path (c1-b2), Hun Min street
(c2-b3).

**The coach asks.** "Where are we in your first picture of this scene?
Could someone who skipped the last scene tell?"

**A note looks like.** "Beat 5 jumps from Meldan to Pafogai Du but opens on
Zei's face. A first picture slot showing the street or the pyramid would
tell the viewer where they are."

## 4. One clear image per beat

**The idea.** Each panel or shot should make one thing clear. Scott McCloud
names *clarity* as the first goal of a comics storyteller, through choices
of moment, frame, image, word and flow (*Making Comics*, 2006). Walter
Murch, writing about film editing, puts the emotion of the moment first
among the reasons for a cut (*In the Blink of an Eye*, 1995). A beat that
asks one image to show two events usually shows neither.

**In the book.** The moment Zei's spaceship turns into Bai's symbol (c4-b132,
c4-b133) is one image. Everything around it in the game is setup.

**The coach asks.** "If this beat had only one picture, what would it show?"

**A note looks like.** "Beat 6's picture slot asks for the explosion and the
escape. Consider splitting it into two beats, or choosing one."

## 5. Narration says what the pictures can't

**The idea.** In a comic, words and pictures work best when each carries
what the other can't. McCloud sorts word and picture combinations and
describes the *interdependent* kind, where they combine to convey an idea
neither could alone (*Understanding Comics*, 1993). Narration that repeats
the picture wastes the reader's attention. Narration that gives time,
thought, a fact the eye can't see, or a contrast with the picture earns its
place. With a 25-word limit per beat, every word counts.

**In the book.** What a picture can't show: that the drone was checked by a
zero-knowledge proof and found harmless (c1-b4), or that his vote has perhaps a
twenty or thirty percent chance of mattering (c1-b70).

**The coach asks.** "What does your narration here tell us that the picture
doesn't?"

**A note looks like.** "Beat 4's narration describes what beat 4's picture
already shows. Those words could carry something the picture can't: a
thought, a time, a cost."

## 6. Introduce a character through what they want

**The idea.** The first thing a viewer should learn about a character is
what they're after. Kurt Vonnegut's advice to writers was that every
character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water (the
preface to *Bagombo Snuff Box*, 1999). Introducing someone through a want
gives the audience a reason to follow them before they know anything else.

**In the book.** Zei is introduced through the game he cares about, a wall
design that won him third place (c2-b12). Soon after, we learn he wants the
championship (c4-b15). Bai is introduced through the prize she wants to take
from him (c4-b73).

**The coach asks.** "When we first see this character, what are they trying
to get?"

**A note looks like.** "Your second character first appears in beat 7
without a want, so the viewer has no reason to watch them yet. What does the
book give them to want at that point?"

## 7. End on a question

**The idea.** A piece, or a part of one, keeps its audience when the end
leaves something open that they want answered. Serial fiction has worked
this way since magazine and newspaper installments. Ira Glass describes the
anecdote in radio storytelling as a sequence of actions that keeps raising
questions the listener wants answered (his talks on storytelling, 2009). A
short piece can end on a question even when the book has already answered
it, if the piece stops just before the answer.

**In the book.** Chapter 1 ends on the anonymous request (c1-b140). Chapter
7 ends with the unopened letter (c7-b144).

**The coach asks.** "What does your last picture leave the viewer wanting
to know?"

**A note looks like.** "The piece ends after the question is answered.
Consider ending one beat earlier, on the message arriving."

## What the coach never does

These come from `docs/adaptation-format.md` ("Models build structure and
images. People write every published word") and the coach's prompt.

- It never writes narration, dialogue, captions, titles or any description
  meant for the piece. That includes example sentences, "something like…",
  rewrites, corrections and fill-ins.
- Its notes say *what* to change and *why*. They never offer the words.
- It may propose an order for the person's own fragments, by their labels,
  and suggest picture slots, saying what each picture should show. A
  picture slot is a plan for an image, not text for the piece.
- It checks world facts against the world guide and the book, cites
  passages by block ID, and never goes past the person's chapter limit.
